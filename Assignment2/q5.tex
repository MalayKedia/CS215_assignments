F_{A}(c)&=P(A<c)\\

&=\sum_{i=1}^{K} P(A < c \mid A = X_i) \cdot P(A = X_i)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{K} P(X_i < c) \cdot P(A = X_i)\\
&= \sum_{i=1}^{K} p_i F_{X_i}(c)
\end{align*}
(Substituting the CDF \( F_{X_i}(c) \) for each Gaussian component)

To find the probability density function (PDF) \( f_A(c) \), we differentiate \( F_A(c) \) with respect to \( c \).
\begin{align*}
f_A(c) &= \frac{d}{dc} F_A(c)\\
&= \sum_{i=1}^{K} p_i \cdot \frac{d}{dc} F_{X_i}(c)\\
&= \sum_{i=1}^{K} p_i \cdot f_{X_i}(c) \hspace{3cm} (\frac{d}{dc} F_{X_i}(c) = f_{X_i}(c))
\end{align*}
The PDF of the GMM variable X is $f_x$, given by $f_X(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{K} p_i \cdot f_{X_i}(x)$, which is exactly same as the one I got from my algorithm. Hence, $\forall u \in R, f_A(u)=f_X(u)$.
\end{solution}

Task D:\\
\begin{solution}
Let \( X \) be a finite discrete random variable taking values \( x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n \) with respective probabilities \( P(X = x_i) = p_i \), for \( i = 1, 2, \dots, n \).\\

The moment generating function (MGF) of \( X \), denoted by \( \phi_X(t) \), is given by:
\[
\phi_X(t) = E[e^{tX}] = \sum_{i=1}^n e^{t x_i} P(X = x_i) = \sum_{i=1}^n e^{t x_i} p_i
\]

Consider the MGF \( \phi_X(t) \). Since the MGF is a power series in \( t \) (the sum of exponentials of the discrete values \( x_i \)), it can be expanded as a Taylor series around \( t = 0 \):
\[
\phi_X(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{E[X^k]}{k!} t^k
\]
The coefficients of this series are the moments of the distribution, which are directly related to the probabilities \( p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n \). If we know the MGF for all \( t \) in some interval, we can recover all the moments of \( X \).

Since \( X \) takes a finite number of discrete values, the MGF is a finite sum of exponentials of \( t \). The exponential function is one-to-one, so the MGF uniquely determines the probabilities \( p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n \). Therefore, the MGF uniquely determines the distribution of \( X \).

#### Step 3: Determining the PDF from the MGF

Given the MGF \( \phi_X(t) = \sum_{i=1}^n e^{t x_i} p_i \), we can determine the probability mass function (PMF) by solving a system of linear equations. For distinct values \( x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n \), the MGF can be written as:
\[
\phi_X(t) = p_1 e^{t x_1} + p_2 e^{t x_2} + \dots + p_n e^{t x_n}
\]
If we know \( \phi_X(t) \) for enough values of \( t \), we can solve for the probabilities \( p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n \) by inverting this system. Therefore, the MGF uniquely determines the PMF.

#### Conclusion for Finite Discrete Case

Thus, for a finite discrete random variable, the MGF uniquely determines the distribution of \( X \). Since the MGF and PDF (or PMF) uniquely determine each other, knowing the MGF is equivalent to knowing the distribution.

### Conclusion about \( X \) and \( Z \)

Since \( Z \) is a weighted sum of independent Gaussian random variables and has the same MGF as \( X \), they share the same distribution (by the theorem). This means that \( Z \) must also follow a Gaussian distribution with the mean and variance derived earlier. 

### Logical Explanation

The reason \( X \) and \( Z \) have the same distribution is that the MGF uniquely characterizes the distribution for both discrete and continuous variables. In this case, since \( Z \) is a sum of independent Gaussian variables, and Gaussian distributions are closed under linear combinations, \( Z \) follows a Gaussian distribution. The MGF, as a summary of all moments, ensures that \( Z \) has the same distribution as the sum of its components.
\end{solution}
